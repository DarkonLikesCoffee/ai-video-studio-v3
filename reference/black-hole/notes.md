## What to take

- **A clearly readable black event-horizon silhouette.** Keep the central void genuinely dark and sharply separated from the luminous material around it. The black hole should remain the unmistakable focal point rather than becoming a bright, solid sphere.
- **A thin, luminous accretion structure that explains the black hole's shape.** The strongest shared motif is a flattened disk crossing the central object. In the more face-on/oblique views, gravitational lensing bends the far side of that disk into a bright arc or near-complete ring above the shadow. Preserve the sense that the ring is distorted light and hot matter wrapping around a dark centre, not a decorative halo pasted on top.
- **Use the two distinct visual treatments as compatible options, not as details to combine indiscriminately.** Frames 01 and 04 favour warm white-gold, amber and orange plasma; frames 02 and 03 favour a cooler white/cyan-blue treatment. A future composition should choose one dominant temperature for its main disk and use the opposite temperature only as restrained contrast, rather than mixing every reference's colors equally.
- **Strong value separation.** Set the brightest disk and lensed rim against near-black space. Retain a broad range from deep black through dark blue/brown, midtone smoky material, saturated orange or blue, and a small near-white highlight core. Let the hottest areas approach white, but keep enough texture and color around them to avoid a featureless clipped band.
- **A readable silhouette and deliberate framing.** The black hole should occupy a substantial portion of the image. Use a horizontal, edge-on disk for a graphic iconic read, or a diagonal disk for a more dynamic, spatially deep composition. Keep the shadow unobscured enough that its circular or oval contour can be read.
- **Layered plasma rather than a flat stripe.** Build the disk from a bright inner band, softer luminous edges, irregular turbulent filaments and a dimmer diffuse envelope. A little unevenness, wispy flow and granular texture makes the material feel energetic and volumetric.
- **A sparse, deep space field.** Small stars and faint nebulous structure can establish scale, especially in frames 01 and 02. Keep them dim and subordinate; the black hole and disk should dominate the contrast and detail budget.
- **Gravitational lensing as a key identifying feature.** Show the far-side disk appearing above and/or below the shadow as a curved luminous arc. The arc can be brighter and more compact than the visible disk itself. It should follow the black-hole contour and feel optically connected to the disk.
- **Scale cues used sparingly.** A subtle star field or a tiny distant object can help the subject feel enormous, as in frame 04, but it must remain secondary and must not compete with the central silhouette.
- **Depth through focus, brightness and layering.** Keep the event-horizon edge and hottest accretion details crispest; allow outer plasma to soften into smoky trails. Separate foreground disk material, lensed rear material and distant stars so they read as different spatial layers.

## What to ignore

- Do not reproduce any one reference literally. Treat these as visual targets for light, shape, palette and framing, not as a request to recreate an exact image or its incidental layout.
- Ignore visible watermarks, signatures, tiny interface-like marks and other source-image artefacts. Frame 03 has a vertical creator mark at the lower-left; frame 04 includes two tiny decorative/star-like marks. They are not part of the black-hole design.
- Do not copy frame 04's small crescent-like object or tiny radial mark as required story elements. They function only as incidental scale/decorative details and are not consistent across the set.
- Avoid making the disk a perfectly uniform, razor-thin line. Frame 02 is intentionally very minimal and distant; copying that exact thinness at a larger scale would weaken the sense of hot, turbulent matter.
- Avoid making every part of the ring equally bright or equally thick. The references vary in brightness and apparent geometry; a uniformly glowing doughnut can look like a neon logo instead of a lensed accretion system.
- Do not add excessive nebula clouds, dense star clusters, colorful galaxies, lens flares or floating debris. The strongest images leave large areas of dark negative space and reserve detail for the disk.
- Do not turn the black-hole shadow into a lit planet, textured sphere or opaque grey ball. Its defining appearance is a dark void with a clean boundary against the surrounding light.
- Do not combine the warm orange-gold treatment and cool blue-white treatment into a rainbow palette. Select a coherent temperature and let any secondary hue support the form.
- Avoid unsupported physical claims or overly literal simulation cues. The stills establish an artistic depiction, not a scientifically complete model of the system.

## Motion and pacing

**Visible evidence:** These are four independent still images, not sequential frames from one continuous shot. They show different disk orientations, scales and color treatments. They do not establish a camera path, rotation direction, orbital speed, particle trajectory or temporal progression. No motion should be described as observed.

**Creative motion suggestions for a future composition:**

- Begin with a slow, controlled camera push toward the black-hole silhouette, or a subtle lateral drift that reveals the disk's depth. Keep the central shadow anchored and readable; avoid fast orbiting that turns the ring into visual noise.
- Let the accretion disk rotate gradually around the shadow. Use layered bands with slightly different apparent speeds and turbulent wisps that drift along the disk, rather than translating the entire disk as a flat image.
- Animate the lensed rear-side arc with a restrained travelling shimmer or gradual brightness variation. Its shape should remain tied to the black-hole contour; avoid a detached ring that spins independently.
- Introduce sparse particles with slow drift and slight parallax. A few brighter specks may follow the disk plane, while distant stars move much more slowly. Avoid a storm of particles or obvious radial explosions.
- Let hot regions pulse gently and irregularly, with small changes in intensity and texture rather than repeated synchronized flashing. The central shadow itself should remain dark and stable.
- Use a measured reveal: establish the dark field, bring up the silhouette and lensed rim, then let the disk's turbulent detail resolve. Hold the fully readable hero composition long enough to register before a slow transition or pull-back.
- Prefer a gradual cross-dissolve, exposure-led reveal, or continuous camera move between views. If changing from a warm to a cool treatment, motivate it with a change in angle, environment or lighting; do not abruptly recolor the same object without visual reason.
- Keep motion calm, immense and deliberate. The subject should feel gravitationally powerful, not explosive or frantic. Any faster movement should be limited to small plasma filaments and particles, not the whole black hole.

## Overall visual direction

Create a solitary, large black hole suspended in deep, nearly black space. Its event-horizon shadow is a clean, near-perfect dark circle or slightly foreshortened oval. A flattened, luminous accretion disk crosses the shadow; gravitational lensing lifts the far side into a bright curved crown above the black centre and may reveal a corresponding lower arc. The disk should have a narrow, intensely bright inner region surrounded by irregular, smoky plasma with visible streaks and granular turbulence.

For a warm version, use white-hot highlights fading through pale gold into amber and burnt orange, with a restrained dark brown/black background. For a cool version, use icy white and pale cyan around the lensed rim, fading into steel-blue and deep navy in the disk. Keep stars faint and sparse. The brightest values belong to the disk and lensing arc, while the event horizon remains the darkest, simplest shape. Compose the object large enough to read immediately, and use either a level horizontal disk for a monumental, symmetrical icon or a diagonal disk for a more dynamic sense of depth. Preserve negative space around the subject and avoid extra objects unless they serve a clear scale or story purpose.

The four references are not a single consistent shot: they range from a warm, near-frontal graphic view to a tiny distant object against a galaxy band, a cool wide horizontal hero view, and a close diagonal warm view. Borrow their common black-hole anatomy and contrast discipline, then select one framing and palette appropriate to the new composition.

- **What is actually visible:** A centred black circular shadow with a bright, almost complete white-gold lensed ring rising above it and a smaller bright arc visible below. A broad, nearly horizontal accretion band crosses the middle of the shadow. The disk is brightest close to the centre and breaks into uneven orange and yellow plasma toward both edges. A dense field of tiny stars fills the black background.
- **Main subject and position:** The black hole is centred and occupies much of the square frame. The disk stretches almost edge to edge, creating a strong horizontal axis.
- **Composition and perspective:** Near-frontal, highly symmetrical presentation. The disk is seen almost edge-on; the upper lensed arc forms a crown over the shadow, while the lower arc reinforces the impression of light wrapping behind it.
- **Dominant colors:** Near-black and dark brown space; white and pale yellow at the hottest rim; gold, amber and orange across the disk.
- **Lighting and atmosphere:** Very high contrast. The rim blooms softly into the surrounding darkness, while the disk has a broad, hot central glow and more irregular, flame-like edges. The star field gives the otherwise graphic composition depth.
- **Distinctive details:** The disk appears as a bright horizontal plasma plane; the upper arc is thick and almost circular, with a crisp dark inner boundary. The lower lensed segment is partially interrupted by the foreground disk.
- **Why this reference is useful:** It is the clearest guide for an iconic, centred black-hole silhouette with a warm palette, readable upper/lower lensing arcs and a strong horizontal disk. Borrow its symmetry and luminous hierarchy, not its exact star density or pixel-level shape.

- **Why this reference is useful:** It shows how to establish enormous scale and a wider cosmic environment while keeping the black hole as a precise point of attention.
